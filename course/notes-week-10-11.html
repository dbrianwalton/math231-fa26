    Section Week 10 and Week 11

    These weeks made the idea of a limit precise and then put it to work. We stated the definition of a limit in terms of an error tolerance \(\epsilon\) for the output and a precision \(\delta\) for the input, and we used particular values of \(\epsilon\) to see when the definition is satisfied. From the definition we built limit rules and used them to justify limits of formulas, one step at a time. We then used tangent lines to define the derivative function and learned to sketch the graph of the derivative from the graph of the original function.
    🔗

    Subsection L4 Illustrating the Limit Definition

    L4.

    I can illustrate the definition of a limit as being satisfied or not satisfied using particular values for epsilon.
    🔗
    🔗
    What do I need to understand?
    • \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) means that for every \(\epsilon \gt 0\) there exists some \(\delta \gt 0\) so that \(|f(x)-L| \lt \epsilon\) whenever \(0 \lt |x-a| \lt \delta\text{.}\) The value \(\epsilon\) is an acceptable tolerance of error for the output, and \(\delta\) is how close the input must be to \(a\) to guarantee that tolerance. Both are distances, so in a context they carry the units of the output and the input.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The inequality \(|f(x)-L| \lt \epsilon\) means \(L-\epsilon \lt f(x) \lt L+\epsilon\text{,}\) a horizontal strip around the line \(y=L\text{.}\) Solving this inequality for \(x\) gives the set of inputs whose outputs are acceptable.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For a particular \(\epsilon\text{,}\) the definition is satisfied when the set of acceptable inputs contains an interval around \(x=a\text{,}\) with the point \(x=a\) itself allowed to be left out. Then \(\delta\) is the smaller of the two distances from \(a\) to the endpoints of that interval, and any smaller positive number also works.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For a particular \(\epsilon\text{,}\) the definition is not satisfied when the set of acceptable inputs does not reach all the way around \(x=a\text{.}\) For example, \(a\) may lie outside the set, or on its edge. Then no positive \(\delta\) works, and the limit statement is false.
      🔗
      🔗
    • One value of \(\epsilon\) that fails is enough to show a limit statement is false. One value of \(\epsilon\) that works is not enough to show it is true, since the definition requires a working \(\delta\) for every \(\epsilon \gt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 16 L4.

    A circuit is powered by a 12 volt battery connected across a resistor of \(R\) ohms. The current in amperes is \(I = \dfrac{12}{R}\text{.}\) With \(R=4\) ohms the current is exactly 3 amperes. The device needs the current to be within \(0.2\) amperes of 3 amperes. How much can the resistance vary from 4 ohms? Explain how this relates to the definition of a limit.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The current must satisfy \(|I-3| \lt 0.2\text{,}\) so \(2.8 \lt I \lt 3.2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We need \(2.8 \lt \dfrac{12}{R} \lt 3.2\) with \(R \gt 0\text{.}\) Taking reciprocals reverses the inequalities, and multiplying by 12 gives \(\dfrac{12}{3.2} \lt R \lt \dfrac{12}{2.8}\text{,}\) which is \(3.75 \lt R \lt \dfrac{30}{7} \approx 4.2857\text{.}\) Alternatively, we find the endpoints by solving \(\dfrac{12}{R} = 2.8\) and \(\dfrac{12}{R} = 3.2\) and use those points as the endpoints of the interval.
    🔗
    The lower endpoint is \(4-3.75=0.25\) below 4 ohms and the upper endpoint is \(\frac{30}{7}-4=\frac{2}{7}\approx 0.2857\) above 4 ohms. The symmetric neighborhood \(|R-4| \lt \delta\) fits inside the acceptable interval when \(\delta \le 0.25\text{,}\) so \(\delta = 0.25\) is the largest value that works, and any smaller positive value also works.
    🔗
    The situation illustrates \(\displaystyle \lim_{R \to 4} \frac{12}{R} = 3\) for the tolerance \(\epsilon = 0.2\text{.}\) If \(0 \lt |R-4| \lt 0.25\text{,}\) then \(\left|\dfrac{12}{R}-3\right| \lt 0.2\text{.}\) Here \(\epsilon=0.2\) is the tolerance on the output current in amperes and \(\delta=0.25\) is the precision on the input resistance in ohms. The definition of the limit makes the stronger promise that a working \(\delta\) exists for every tolerance \(\epsilon \gt 0\text{,}\) not just \(0.2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection L5 Justifying Limits with Limit Rules

    L5.

    I can apply limit rules according to rules of deductive reasoning to justify the value of a limit based on formulas.
    🔗
    🔗
    What do I need to understand?
    • A justification is a sequence of true limit statements, each followed by the reason it is true. The reason is either a named rule (an elementary limit or a limit of a combination) or “given”.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Deconstruct the formula to plan the justification: find the last operation, then repeat for each piece until every piece is a constant, the identity function, or a linear function. Write the justification in the opposite order, starting from those elementary pieces and building up to the full formula.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Each combination rule requires that the limits of its parts are already known. In each step, name the earlier step numbers that you are combining. Powers of \(x\) are justified by repeated use of the product rule.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The quotient rule and the reciprocal rule require that the limit of the denominator is not zero. Write down the limit of the denominator and note that it is nonzero before using the rule. If it is zero, the rule cannot be used and a different method, such as factoring and cancelling, is needed.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A given limit such as \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M\) is a place to start, with the reason “given”. It does not say that \(g(a)=M\text{,}\) and \(g(a)\) may not even exist. We may also be given a limit of some combination, such as \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} \frac{g(x)}{x-a}\text{,}\) which cannot be separated since division by \(x-a\) would have denominator 0.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 17 L5.

    Suppose \(f(x)\) is a function for which \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} f(x)(x-4) = 5\text{.}\) Find and justify the limit
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{2f(x)(x-4)}{x^2-2x+3} \end{equation*}
    using limit rules involving either elementary limits or combinations of limits that have already been demonstrated.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The last operation is a quotient. The numerator is a constant multiple of the product \(f(x)(x-4)\text{,}\) whose limit is given, so we treat that product as a single piece and do not need to know \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} f(x)\) by itself. The denominator is built from \(x^2\text{,}\) \(2x\text{,}\) and a constant.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} f(x)(x-4) = 5\) (given).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} 2f(x)(x-4) = 2(5) = 10\) (constant multiple rule with Step 1).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} x = 4\) (limit of the identity function).
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} x^2 = (4)(4) = 16\) (product rule with Step 3 used for both factors).
      🔗
      🔗
    5. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} -2x+3 = -2(4)+3 = -5\) (limit of a linear function3).
      🔗
      🔗
    6. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} (x^2-2x+3) = 16+-5 = 11\) (sum rule with Steps 4 and 5).
      🔗
      🔗
    7. \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{2f(x)(x-4)}{x^2-2x+3} = \frac{10}{11}\) (quotient rule with Steps 2 and 6, which applies because the limit of the denominator, \(11\text{,}\) is not zero).
      🔗
      🔗

    Subsection D3 Sketching the Derivative Graph

    D3.

    I can create (sketch) a graph of a derivative function based on the original function’s graph.
    🔗
    🔗
    What do I need to understand?
    • The output \(f'(a)\) is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f(x)\) at \(x=a\text{.}\) The graph of \(f'(x)\) reports slopes, not heights, so it does not look like a copy of the graph of \(f(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Where \(f(x)\) has a horizontal tangent line, such as a local maximum or local minimum, \(f'(x)=0\text{.}\) Mark these \(x\)-values first as the places where the graph of \(f'(x)\) meets the \(x\)-axis.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Where \(f(x)\) is increasing, the slopes are positive, so the graph of \(f'(x)\) is above the \(x\)-axis. Where \(f(x)\) is decreasing, the slopes are negative, so the graph of \(f'(x)\) is below the \(x\)-axis.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The steeper the graph of \(f(x)\text{,}\) the farther the graph of \(f'(x)\) is from the \(x\)-axis. Between two horizontal tangents, the graph of \(f(x)\) is steepest near an inflection point, so that is where \(f'(x)\) reaches a local maximum or local minimum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Build the sketch in order: mark the zeros, decide the sign on each interval, locate the steepest points, and then connect everything with a smooth curve. Estimates of slopes are fine, but the zeros, signs, and the places where \(f'(x)\) is largest or smallest must be correct.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 18 D3.

    The graph of \(f(x)\) is shown for \(-3 \le x \le 4\text{.}\) Each grid square is 1 unit wide and 1 unit tall.
    🔗
    Sketch the graph of \(f'(x)\) on the axes below. Mark the \(x\)-values where \(f'(x)=0\) and your graph should show clearly where \(f'(x)\) is increasing and decreasing, with reasonable estimates for actual values at extremes.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The graph of \(f(x)\) has horizontal tangent lines at \(x=-2\text{,}\) \(x=0\text{,}\) and \(x=3\text{,}\) so \(f'(x)=0\) at those three \(x\)-values.
    🔗
    The graph of \(f(x)\) is increasing on \((-\infty,-2)\) and \((0,3)\text{,}\) so \(f'(x)\) is positive there. It is decreasing on \((-2,0)\) and \((3,\infty)\text{,}\) so \(f'(x)\) is negative there. The graph of \(f'(x)\) therefore crosses the \(x\)-axis at each of the three \(x\)-values, and it has the shape of a cubic with a negative leading coefficient.
    🔗
    The graph of \(f(x)\) is steepest between the horizontal tangent lines near its inflection points, at about \(x=-1.1\) and \(x=1.8\text{.}\) On \((-2,0)\) the graph is falling, so \(f'(x)\) has a local minimum near \(x=-1.1\) with a slope of about \(-1.5\text{.}\) On \((0,3)\) the graph is rising, so \(f'(x)\) has a local maximum near \(x=1.8\) with a slope of about \(3\text{.}\) The graph of \(f(x)\) is rising fairly steeply at \(x=-3\) and falling steeply at \(x=4\text{,}\) so \(f'(x)\) is about \(6.75\) at \(x=-3\) and \(-9\) at \(x=4\text{.}\)
