    Worksheet Using Limit Rules
    

    Until now we have found limits from graphs, tables, and algebraic simplification. Proving a limit directly from the epsilon-delta definition is far too slow to do for every formula we meet. Today we learn to justify a limit by listing the limit rules that build it, one step at a time.
    🔗
    The rules below are the only tools we need. Keep this page handy, because every justification today is a list of true limit statements, each with the name of the rule that makes it true.
    🔗

    1. Section 1: The Limit Rules and Deconstructing a Formula.

    Elementary Limits.

    • Limit of a Constant Function: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} c = c\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of the Identity Function: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} x = a\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of a Linear Function: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} (mx+b) = ma+b\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Limits of Combinations.

    Prior to each limit of combination, we must first know the limits of the parts that are being combined, which we represent as \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} u(x) = L\) and \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} v(x) = M\text{.}\)
    • Limit of a Constant Multiple: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} [k u(x)] = kL\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of a Reciprocal: If \(L \ne 0\text{,}\) then \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} \left[\frac{1}{u(x)}\right] = \frac{1}{L}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of a Sum: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} [u(x)+v(x)] = L+M\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of a Difference: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} [u(x)-v(x)] = L-M\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of a Product: \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} [u(x)\, v(x)] = LM\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of a Quotient: If \(M \ne 0\text{,}\) then \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} \left[\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\right] = \frac{L}{M}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Limit of a Continuous Composition: If \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} v(x) = M\) and \(f(u)\) is continuous at \(u=M\text{,}\) then \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to a} (f \circ g)(x) = \lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = f(M)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    We can justify limits without writing epsilon-delta proofs if we use these rules instead. The limits of the elementary functions (constant, identity, and linear) were proved, so we justify such a limit by identifying the elementary function in play and naming the corresponding rule.
    🔗
    All of the more complicated algebraic functions are built from elementary functions by combining them with arithmetic or composition. Powers of the independent variable are repeated multiplication, so they are justified by repeated use of the product rule. We can therefore justify a limit by identifying how the formula is constructed and naming the appropriate limit rules.
    🔗
    Notice that each combination rule requires that we already know the limits of the component parts before we can combine them. In practice we think through a formula starting from its last operation. A logical justification, however, goes the other way: we first list the elementary pieces and then combine them one step at a time.
    🔗
    Our first example is \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} \left(3x^2+2x-5\right)\text{.}\) To begin, we deconstruct the formula by asking what the last operation is, and then doing the same for each piece.
    🔗

    (a)

    The formula \(3x^2+2x-5\) is a sum. Which two formulas are being added? Which of them is a linear function, so that we can use an elementary limit?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The other formula in the sum is \(3x^2\text{.}\) Explain why it is a constant multiple of \(x^2\text{.}\) What is the constant?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Explain why \(x^2\) is a product of two copies of the identity function.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Section 2: Building a Justification for a Sum.

    A justification is a numbered list of limit statements that we know are true, each followed by the reason. We start from the elementary pieces and work up to the full formula. For \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} \left(3x^2+2x-5\right)\) we begin with two elementary limits, and then each new step uses results from earlier steps.
    🔗
    Write each step in the form “limit statement, then (rule used)”. In the combination steps, also name the earlier step numbers that you are combining.
    🔗

    (a)

    Step 1. Write the limit of the identity function as \(x \to 2\text{,}\) and name the rule.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Step 2. Write the limit of the linear formula \(2x-5\) as \(x \to 2\text{,}\) and name the rule.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Step 3. Use the product rule with \(u(x)=x\) and \(v(x)=x\) and Step 1 to find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Step 4. Use the constant multiple rule with Step 3 to find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} 3x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Step 5. Use the sum rule with \(u(x)=3x^2\) from Step 4 and \(v(x)=2x-5\) from Step 2 to find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} \left(3x^2+2x-5\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Section 3: Building a Justification for a Quotient.

    Now consider
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{3x^2(2x-5)}{x^2-3x+2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The last operation is a quotient, so we will eventually use the quotient rule.
    🔗

    Check the Denominator.

    The quotient rule only applies when the limit of the denominator is not zero. Before using the quotient rule, we must write down the limit of the denominator and note that it is nonzero. If it is zero, the quotient rule cannot be used, and we need a different method, such as the factoring and cancelling from the previous activity.
    🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    Deconstruct the formula.
    🔗
    1. The numerator \(3x^2(2x-5)\) is a product. What are the two factors? Which factor is a linear function, and which is a constant multiple of a power?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The denominator \(x^2-3x+2\) is a difference. Which two formulas are being subtracted? (Hint: group \(3x-2\) together.) Which of them is linear?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Start the justification with the elementary limits as \(x \to 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Step 1. The identity function.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Step 2. The linear formula \(2x-5\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Step 3. The linear formula \(3x-2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Build the limit of the numerator.
    🔗
    1. Step 4. Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} x^2\) (product rule).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Step 5. Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} 3x^2\) (constant multiple rule).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Step 6. Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} 3x^2(2x-5)\) (product rule).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Step 7. Build the limit of the denominator. Use the difference rule with \(u(x)=x^2\) from Step 4 and \(v(x)=3x-2\) from Step 3 to find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} \left(x^2-3x+2\right)\text{.}\) Is this limit nonzero?
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Step 8. Use the quotient rule with Steps 6 and 7 to find the original limit. State the condition on the denominator that lets you use the rule.
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Find the limit of the denominator \(x^2-3x+2\) as \(x \to 1\) and as \(x \to 2\text{.}\) Can the quotient rule be used for the original fraction at those \(x\)-values? What would you try instead?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Section 4: Starting from a Given Limit.

    Sometimes a problem does not give us a formula for every piece. Consider
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{3\, g(x) \left(x^2+4\right)}{(2x+1)^2} \end{equation*}
    where we are told that \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} g(x) = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗

    A Given Limit Is Not a Function Value.

    We are not saying that \(g(x)=4\text{.}\) We are not even saying that \(g(1)=4\text{,}\) since \(g\) might be undefined at \(x=1\) or might have a different value there.
    🔗
    We are simply treating the statement \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} g(x) = 4\) as if it were a new elementary limit that we get to start with, because the problem gave it to us. In a justification, the reason for this step is “given”.
    🔗
    🔗

    (a)

    Deconstruct the formula.
    🔗
    1. What is the last operation? Name the numerator and the denominator.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The numerator is a product. What are its two factors? Which factor involves a constant multiple of \(g(x)\text{,}\) and which is a sum?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The denominator \((2x+1)^2\) is a product of two copies of what linear formula?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Start the justification with the limits we get to begin with, as \(x \to 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Step 1. The limit of \(g(x)\text{.}\) (What is the reason for this step?)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Step 2. The identity function.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Step 3. The linear formula \(2x+1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Build the limit of the numerator.
    🔗
    1. Step 4. Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} 3\,g(x)\) (constant multiple rule with Step 1).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Step 5. Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} x^2\) (product rule with Step 2).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Step 6. Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} \left(x^2+4\right)\) (sum rule with Step 5 and the constant function).
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Step 7. Find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} 3\,g(x)\left(x^2+4\right)\) (product rule with Steps 4 and 6).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Step 8. Build the limit of the denominator. Use the product rule with Step 3 used for both factors to find \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 1} (2x+1)^2\text{.}\) Is this limit nonzero?
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Step 9. Use the quotient rule with Steps 7 and 8 to find the original limit. Simplify your answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
