    Section Week 12 and Week 13

    During these weeks, we used the derivative to describe the shape of a graph and then used that shape to answer optimization questions. We used the sign of the first derivative to find where a function increases and decreases, and the sign of the second derivative to find where it is concave up and concave down. We also learned to invert the derivative process to find antiderivatives of polynomials, and we used a known function value to determine the unknown constant. Finally, we found the global extremes a function reaches on an interval.
    🔗

    Subsection A1 Analyzing a Function with Its Derivatives

    A1.

    I can analyze a function using the derivative to determine its intervals of monotonicity and concavity, including analysis based on the graph of the derivative.
    🔗
    🔗
    What do I need to understand?
    • The values of \(x\) where \(f'(x)=0\) or \(f'(x)\) is not defined are called the critical points of \(f(x)\text{.}\) They are the potential points where \(f(x)\) might change direction.
      🔗
      🔗
    • We use sign analysis of \(f'(x)\text{,}\) with the critical points of \(f(x)\) as the dividing points, to find the intervals on which \(f(x)\) is increasing or decreasing. Where \(f'(x) \gt 0\text{,}\) the function is increasing. Where \(f'(x) \lt 0\text{,}\) the function is decreasing.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The correct phrase is “\(f(x)\) is increasing on the interval” or “\(f(x)\) is decreasing on the interval”. Increasing and decreasing are based on comparing any two \(x\)-values chosen from the specified set, so they describe an interval and not an individual point.
      🔗
      🔗
    • We use sign analysis of \(f''(x)\text{,}\) with the critical points of \(f'(x)\) as the dividing points, to find the intervals on which \(f(x)\) is concave up or concave down.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A function is concave up when the slope itself is increasing, so when \(f''(x) \gt 0\text{.}\) A function is concave down when the slope is decreasing, so when \(f''(x) \lt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • A local maximum occurs at a point where \(f(x)\) is increasing on the left and decreasing on the right. A local minimum occurs at a point where \(f(x)\) is decreasing on the left and increasing on the right. In terms of the derivative, \(f'(x)\) changes sign at the point.
      🔗
      🔗
    • An inflection point occurs at a point where the concavity changes. It is a place where \(f''(x)\) changes sign, so \(f''(x)=0\) alone is not enough.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When we are given the graph of \(f'(x)\) instead of a formula, we read the same information from the graph. Where the graph of \(f'(x)\) is above the \(x\)-axis, \(f(x)\) is increasing, and where it is below the \(x\)-axis, \(f(x)\) is decreasing. Where the graph of \(f'(x)\) is rising, \(f(x)\) is concave up, and where it is falling, \(f(x)\) is concave down. The local maximums and local minimums of the graph of \(f'(x)\) are the inflection points of \(f(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 19 A1.

    A function \(f(x)\) has a derivative
    \begin{align*} f'(x) \amp = -2x^3 -4x^2 + 30x = -2x(x+5)(x-3). \end{align*}
    Using information about the derivatives \(f'\) and \(f''\text{,}\) describe the intervals on which \(f(x)\) is increasing, decreasing, concave up, and concave down, and identify the \(x\)-coordinates of any local extremes.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The derivative is defined everywhere, so the critical points of \(f(x)\) are the zeros of the factored form \(f'(x) = -2x(x+5)(x-3)\text{:}\) \(x=-5\text{,}\) \(x=0\text{,}\) and \(x=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We test the sign of \(f'(x)\) on each interval, using the factored form to keep the arithmetic simple.
    🔗
    Interval \((-\infty,-5)\) \((-5,0)\) \((0,3)\) \((3,\infty)\)
    Sign of \(f'(x)\) \(+\) \(-\) \(+\) \(-\)
    Behavior of \(f(x)\) increasing decreasing increasing decreasing
    So \(f(x)\) is increasing on \((-\infty,-5)\) and on \((0,3)\text{,}\) and \(f(x)\) is decreasing on \((-5,0)\) and on \((3,\infty)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The function changes from increasing to decreasing at \(x=-5\) and at \(x=3\text{,}\) so \(f(x)\) has a local maximum at each of those \(x\)-values. The function changes from decreasing to increasing at \(x=0\text{,}\) so \(f(x)\) has a local minimum at \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For concavity we differentiate the expanded form again: \(f''(x) = -6x^2-8x+30 = -2(3x^2+4x-15) = -2(3x-5)(x+3)\text{.}\) The critical points of \(f'(x)\) are the zeros of \(f''(x)\text{,}\) which are \(x=-3\) and \(x=\frac{5}{3}\text{.}\) Testing the sign of \(f''(x)\) gives the following sign chart.
    🔗
    Interval \((-\infty,-3)\) \(\left(-3,\frac{5}{3}\right)\) \(\left(\frac{5}{3},\infty\right)\)
    Sign of \(f''(x)\) \(-\) \(+\) \(-\)
    Concavity of \(f(x)\) concave down concave up concave down
    So \(f(x)\) is concave up on \(\left(-3,\frac{5}{3}\right)\) and concave down on \((-\infty,-3)\) and on \(\left(\frac{5}{3},\infty\right)\text{.}\) The concavity changes at \(x=-3\) and at \(x=\frac{5}{3}\text{,}\) so these are the \(x\)-coordinates of the inflection points.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A2 Antiderivatives with an Initial Condition

    A2.

    I can find an antiderivative for a polynomial with initial condition.
    🔗
    🔗
    What do I need to understand?
    • The reverse power rule undoes the power rule for derivatives. For \(n \ne -1\text{,}\)
      \begin{align*} \int dx\left[ a x^n \right] \amp = \frac{a}{n+1} x^{n+1} + C \end{align*}
      The exponent goes up by 1, and the coefficient is divided by the new exponent. A constant term is a power with \(n=0\text{,}\) so the antiderivative of \(7\) is \(7x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • The sum rule and constant multiple rule let us work one term at a time. This works as long as the expression only involves addition and multiplication by constants. A product such as \((x+1)(x+3)\) must be expanded into terms first.
      🔗
      🔗
    • An antiderivative always has an unknown constant \(C\text{,}\) because functions that differ only by a constant have the same derivative. Knowing \(f'(x)\) alone does not determine a single function \(f(x)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • To find the constant, evaluate the antiderivative formula at the given input, set the result equal to the given output value, and solve for \(C\text{.}\) Then write the final formula with the value of \(C\) included.
      🔗
      🔗
    • You can check an antiderivative by taking its derivative. The result should be the original \(f'(x)\text{,}\) and evaluating your final formula should match the given value.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 20 A2.

    Find the function \(f(x)\) such that \(f'(x) = 10x^4 - 6x^2 + 2\) and \(f(1) = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We use the reverse power rule on each term.
    \begin{align*} f(x) \amp = \int dx\left[ 10x^4 - 6x^2 + 2 \right]\\ \amp = \frac{10}{5}x^5 - \frac{6}{3}x^3 + 2x + C\\ \amp = 2x^5 - 2x^3 + 2x + C \end{align*}
    🔗
    Now we use \(f(1)=4\) to find \(C\text{.}\)
    \begin{align*} f(1) \amp = 2(1)^5 - 2(1)^3 + 2(1) + C = 2 + C\\ 2 + C \amp = 4\\ C \amp = 2 \end{align*}
    🔗
    Therefore \(f(x) = 2x^5 - 2x^3 + 2x + 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection A4 Global Extreme Values on an Interval

    A4.

    I can find the global extreme values of a function on a specified interval.
    🔗
    🔗
    What do I need to understand?
    • A global maximum on an interval is the highest value that the function actually takes on that interval, and a global minimum is the lowest value it actually takes. A local extreme only has to be highest or lowest nearby, so a local maximum is not always a global maximum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • For a continuous function on a closed interval \([a,b]\text{,}\) the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees that both a global maximum and a global minimum exist. Polynomials are continuous, so this applies to every polynomial on a closed interval.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A global extreme can only occur at a critical point inside the interval or at one of the two end points. Find the critical points of \(f(x)\) by solving \(f'(x)=0\text{,}\) and keep only the ones that lie in the interval. A critical point outside the interval is ignored.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Evaluate the original function \(f(x)\text{,}\) not \(f'(x)\text{,}\) at each critical point in the interval and at both end points. The largest of these values is the global maximum and the smallest is the global minimum.
      🔗
      🔗
    • An answer has two parts: the extreme value, which is a \(y\)-value, and the \(x\)-value where it occurs. An extreme can occur at an end point, and the same extreme value can occur at more than one \(x\)-value.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 21 A4.

    Find the global maximum and the global minimum of \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 2\) on the interval \([-2,2)\text{,}\) if they exist. For each one that exists, state the value of the extreme and the \(x\)-value where it occurs.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The interval is not closed, so the Extreme Value Theorem does not guarantee that both extremes exist. We find the range of the function on the interval and then decide.
    🔗
    We find the critical points: \(f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x-3)(x+1)\text{.}\) The derivative is zero at \(x=3\) and \(x=-1\text{.}\) Only \(x=-1\) lies in \([-2,2)\text{,}\) so we ignore \(x=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The end point \(x=-2\) is included, so we use the function value \(f(-2)=0\text{.}\) The end point \(x=2\) is not included, so we cannot use \(f(2)\text{.}\) Instead we use a limit.
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) \amp = \left. (x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 2) \right|_{x=2} = 8 - 12 - 18 + 2 = -20 \end{align*}
    🔗
    \(x\) Why it matters \(y\)-value
    \(-2\) included end point \(0\)
    \(-1\) critical point \(7\)
    \(2\) excluded end point \(-20\) (as a limit only)
    The highest value is \(7\text{,}\) and it is reached at \(x=-1\text{.}\) The lowest value, \(-20\text{,}\) is only a limit as \(x\) approaches \(2\) from the left. The range is \((-20,7]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The global maximum is \(7\text{,}\) which occurs at \(x=-1\text{.}\) There is no global minimum. The function is decreasing on \((-1,2)\text{,}\) so for every \(x\) in the interval there is another \(x\)-value closer to \(2\) with a lower output, and the value \(-20\) is never reached.
    🔗
