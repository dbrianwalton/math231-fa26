    Worksheet Velocity, Acceleration, and Concavity
    

    So far we have thought of the derivative \(f'(x)\) graphically, as the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f(x)\text{.}\) A slope computes the ratio of a change in the output to a change in the input, which means it measures how fast the output is changing with respect to the input. In this activity we give the derivative a physical meaning in the setting of motion, where a rate of change is its own measurable quantity (velocity).
    🔗
    Because the derivative (velocity) is itself a measurable function of time, we can talk about its derivative. That gives us a way to describe how the slope itself is changing, which is exactly what concavity means. Throughout this activity, we allow the time \(t\) to be any real number, where negative values mean times before the clock was started.
    🔗

    1. Section 1: Rate of change as velocity.

    Suppose an object moves along a straight line, such as a road or a number line. It could be horizontal like a track or vertical like a ball thrown up into the air. Let \(s(t)\) measure the object’s position at time \(t\text{.}\) The slope of the graph of \(s(t)\) gives the rate of change in position with respect to the change in time. If position is measured in meters and time in seconds, then the slope has units of meters per second. That should be a familiar kind of quantity.
    🔗

    Velocity.

    If \(s(t)\) is the position of an object at time \(t\text{,}\) then the derivative
    \begin{gather*} v(t) = s'(t) \end{gather*}
    is the velocity of the object. Graphically, \(v(t)\) gives the slope of the graph of \(s(t)\) at each value \(t\text{.}\) Physically, \(v(t)\) is the rate of change of position, with units of distance per unit of time.
    🔗
    🔗
    Velocity has a sign, and the sign tells us the direction of motion. If \(v(t)\) is positive, then the object is moving toward the positive direction, so \(s(t)\) is increasing. If \(v(t)\) is negative, then the position \(s(t)\) is decreasing and the object is moving in the negative direction. If \(v(t)=0\text{,}\) then the object is instantaneously not moving in either direction. This typically corresponds to a critical point of position where it might change direction.
    🔗

    A cyclist rides along a straight road. Let \(s(t)\) be the cyclist’s distance in meters east of a signpost, \(t\) seconds after a stopwatch is started.

    3. Section 3: Acceleration and the second derivative.

    The velocity \(v(t)\) is itself a function of time, so it has a derivative too. The rate of change of velocity measures the change in velocity per unit of time. With velocity in meters per second and time in seconds, its units are meters per second per second, written m/s\(^2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Acceleration and the Second Derivative.

    The rate of change of velocity is called the acceleration of the object:
    \begin{gather*} a(t) = v'(t)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    In calculus, the derivative of a derivative is called the second derivative. Since \(v(t)=s'(t)\text{,}\) acceleration is the second derivative of position with respect to time,
    \begin{gather*} a(t) = s''(t)\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The number of primes tells us how many derivatives have been taken.
    🔗
    🔗
    We can apply the same tools to velocity that we applied to position. The solutions of \(v'(t)=0\) are the critical points of the velocity. Sign analysis of \(a(t)=v'(t)\) tells us where \(v(t)\) is increasing (where \(a(t)\gt 0\)) and where \(v(t)\) is decreasing (where \(a(t)\lt 0\)).
    🔗
    Note: Velocity has a sign that depends on the direction of motion. So “\(v(t)\) is increasing” means that the value of \(v(t)\) moves to the right on the number line, as when it goes from \(-5\) to \(-1\) or from \(-1\) to \(3\text{.}\) It is not about getting faster or slower. For example, a velocity that goes from \(-1\) to \(-5\) is decreasing, even though the object is moving faster.
    🔗

    4. Section 4: Concavity and sketching position from velocity.

    In the last task you described how the slope of the graph of \(s(t)\) was changing. That is the idea behind concavity, and it applies to any function, not just to position.
    🔗

    Concavity and the Second Derivative.

    Concavity is defined by how the slope is changing. A function \(f(x)\) is concave up on an interval if the derivative \(f'(x)\) is increasing on that interval. A function \(f(x)\) is concave down on an interval if the derivative \(f'(x)\) is decreasing on that interval.
    🔗
    Because \(f''(x)\) is what we use to find where \(f'(x)\) is increasing or decreasing, sign analysis of \(f''(x)\) determines the concavity:
    🔗
    A point where the concavity changes is an inflection point, so it occurs where \(f''(x)\) changes sign.
    🔗
    🔗
    For position, \(f''\) is the acceleration. Together, the sign of the velocity \(v(t)=s'(t)\) and the sign of the acceleration \(a(t)=s''(t)\) describe the full shape of the graph of \(s(t)\text{.}\)
