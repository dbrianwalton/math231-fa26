    Worksheet Limits as Error Tolerance
    

    In the real world, a formula predicts a quantity from an input, which we can only control to a certain precision. A machine cannot cut a side length of exactly 2 meters, and a driver cannot hold a speed of exactly 60 miles per hour. The question that matters in practice is this: how precisely must we control the input so that the predicted output stays within an acceptable error?
    🔗
    In the activity “The Definition of a Limit” we gave the limit definition and saw that \(\epsilon\) is an acceptable tolerance of error in the output and \(\delta\) is how close the input must be to guarantee it. Today we give that definition a physical meaning in two situations, and we see that the answer to the practical question is exactly what a limit statement says.
    🔗

    1. Section 1: What the Tiles Were Telling Us.

    In the activity “The Definition of a Limit” you worked out the specific example \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2} x^2 = 4\) with \(\epsilon = 0.1\text{.}\) That example has a physical meaning. The area covered by a square tile is \(A = s^2\text{,}\) where \(s\) is the side length in meters. A tile with side \(s=2\) has exactly \(4\ \mathrm{m}^2\) of area, but a cutting machine can only control the side length so precisely. Suppose the manufacturer insists that every tile have an area within \(0.1\ \mathrm{m}^2\) of \(4\ \mathrm{m}^2\text{.}\) The side lengths you found that work are about \(1.9748 \lt s \lt 2.0248\text{,}\) which is roughly \(0.0252\) below 2 and \(0.0248\) above 2. Because the neighborhood \(|s-2| \lt \delta\) is symmetric, the machine is guaranteed to be safe only if it stays within the smaller distance, about \(0.0248\) meters, of the target. Any smaller \(\delta\) also works.
    🔗

    For the tile problem with \(a=2\) and \(L=4\text{,}\) answer the following.
    1. What does \(\epsilon = 0.1\) measure, and in what units?

    2. Section 2: Larger Tiles.

    The manufacturer also produces larger tiles with target side length \(s=3\) meters and target area \(9\ \mathrm{m}^2\text{.}\) The area must again be within \(\epsilon = 0.1\ \mathrm{m}^2\) of the target. This corresponds to the limit statement \(\displaystyle \lim_{s \to 3} s^2 = 9\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Find an input tolerance \(\delta\) that works for \(\displaystyle \lim_{s \to 3} s^2 = 9\) with \(\epsilon = 0.1\text{.}\) Show the interval of acceptable areas, the interval of acceptable side lengths, and the two distances from \(s=3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    1. Explain what your \(\delta\) means for the person running the cutting machine.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compare it to the \(\delta\) for the 2 meter tile. Which tile needs the more precise cut, and why, in terms of how much the area changes when the side length changes a little?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice what the definition of the limit promises. Regardless of how small the required tolerance \(\epsilon\) is chosen, you will be able to find a precision \(\delta\) for the input that achieves it. We tested only \(\epsilon = 0.1\text{,}\) but the claim is about every \(\epsilon \gt 0\text{.}\) If there is even one \(\epsilon\) for which no \(\delta\) works, then the limit statement was not valid.
    🔗
    🔗

    3. Section 3: Travel Time and Speed.

    A delivery truck makes a trip of 120 miles at a constant speed of \(v\) miles per hour. The travel time in hours is \(t = \dfrac{120}{v}\text{.}\) At \(v=60\) mph the trip takes exactly 2 hours. The company needs the arrival time to be within \(0.1\) hours of 2 hours. How much can the driver’s speed vary from 60 mph? Explain how this relates to the definition of a limit.
