    Worksheet Derivatives of Polynomials and Critical Points
    

    Last time we defined the derivative \(f'(x)\) as the function whose output at each \(x\)-value is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f(x)\text{.}\) We found it by drawing tangent lines and estimating slopes, which is slow and imprecise.
    🔗
    Today we use technology to look for patterns in the derivatives of polynomials, and we state those patterns as rules that give exact formulas. Then we use the formulas to find the \(x\)-values where the graph of \(f(x)\) has a horizontal tangent line.
    🔗

    2. Section 2: Derivative formulas.

    Taking a derivative is an operation we perform on a formula, just as taking a limit is. We give it an operator symbol.
    🔗

    The Differentiation Operator.

    The notation \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[ u \right]\) means “the derivative of the formula \(u\) with respect to \(x\)”. If \(f(x)\) is a function, then
    \begin{gather*} f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left[ f(x) \right]\text{.} \end{gather*}
    The operator acts on a formula, so we can use it without giving the formula a function name.
    🔗
    🔗
    What you saw in Desmos can be stated as rules. These are observed patterns for now. We will prove them once we have the definition of the derivative.
    🔗

    The Power Rule.

    For any positive integer \(n\text{,}\)
    \begin{gather*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[ x^n \right] = n x^{n-1}\text{.} \end{gather*}
    In particular \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[ x \right] = 1\text{.}\) For a constant \(k\text{,}\) the graph of \(y=k\) is a horizontal line, so \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[ k \right] = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    The Sum Rule and the Constant Multiple Rule.

    Let \(u\) and \(v\) be formulas and let \(k\) be a constant.
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[ u + v \right] \amp = \frac{d}{dx}\left[ u \right] + \frac{d}{dx}\left[ v \right]\\ \frac{d}{dx}\left[ k\,u \right] \amp = k\,\frac{d}{dx}\left[ u \right]\text{.} \end{align*}
    Subtraction is covered by these two rules, since \(u - v = u + (-1)v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We often use both the sum and constant multiple rules in a single step. When combined, it is often called the linearity of the derivative. Suppose \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are any constants and \(u\) and \(v\) represent formulas:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[ c_1 u + c_2 v \right] = c_1 \frac{d}{dx}[u] + c_2 \frac{d}{dx}[v] \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Together, these let us differentiate any polynomial one term at a time.
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dx}\left[ 5x^4 - 3x^2 + 7x - 2 \right] \amp = 5\,\frac{d}{dx}\left[ x^4 \right] - 3\,\frac{d}{dx}\left[ x^2 \right] + 7\,\frac{d}{dx}\left[ x \right] - \frac{d}{dx}\left[ 2 \right]\\ \amp = 5(4x^3) - 3(2x) + 7(1) - 0\\ \amp = 20x^3 - 6x + 7\text{.} \end{align*}
    With practice you will skip the middle line and write the answer directly. Notice that the degree of the polynomial dropped by one, as you observed.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle \frac{d}{dx}\left[ 6x^5 - 4x^3 \right]\)

    3. Section 3: Critical points.

    In the last task you found that \(f'(4)=0\text{,}\) so the graph of \(f(x)\) has a horizontal tangent line at \(x=4\text{.}\) The \(x\)-values where the slope is zero, or where there is no slope exists, are the places where the behavior of a graph can change direction. We give them a name.
    🔗

    Critical Points.

    A critical point of \(f(x)\) is an \(x\)-value \(x=a\) in the domain of \(f(x)\) where either \(f'(a)=0\) or \(f'(a)\) does not exist.
    🔗
    🔗
    The graph with the corner from last class has a critical point at its corner, because the tangent line does not exist there. For a polynomial, however, \(f'(x)\) is again a polynomial, so it exists for every \(x\text{.}\) That means the critical points of a polynomial are exactly the roots of \(f'(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Finding Critical Points of a Polynomial.

    1. Compute \(f'(x)\) using the derivative rules.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Factor \(f'(x)\) completely. Pull out the greatest common factor first, including any constant, and then factor what remains.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. A product is zero only when one of its factors is zero. Set each factor containing \(x\) equal to zero and solve.
      • A constant factor is never zero, so it adds no roots.
        🔗
        🔗
      • If a quadratic factor does not factor, use the quadratic formula.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    For \(f(x)=x^3-6x^2+5\) we found \(f'(x)=3x^2-12x\text{,}\) and so
    \begin{align*} f'(x) \amp = 3x^2-12x = 3x(x-4)\\ 3x(x-4) = 0 \amp \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=0 \text{ or } x=4\text{.} \end{align*}
    The critical points of \(f(x)\) are \(x=0\) and \(x=4\text{.}\) Keep the factored form of \(f'(x)\text{.}\) Next we will use it to decide where \(f'(x)\) is positive and negative.
