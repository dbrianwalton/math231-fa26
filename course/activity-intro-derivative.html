    Worksheet Tangent Lines and the Derivative
    

    A line has one slope, and that slope is the same everywhere along it. A curved graph is different: it is steeper in some places and shallower in others, and sometimes it is perfectly horizontal. So “the slope of the graph” only makes sense for each particular point.
    🔗
    In this activity we measure the slope of a graph at a point by drawing a tangent line there. Then we collect those slopes into a brand new function, one that takes an \(x\)-value and reports how steep the graph is at that \(x\)-value. This new function is the central object of the rest of the course.
    🔗

    1. Section 1: Zooming in on a curve.

    Imagine zooming in on a smooth curve at one of its points. The closer we look, the straighter the curve appears. The line that the curve turns into is the line we want.
    🔗

    Tangent Line.

    A tangent line to the graph of \(f(x)\) at \(x=a\) is a line that passes through the point \((a, f(a))\) and that looks like the graph of \(f(x)\) when we zoom in close enough to that point.
    🔗
    🔗
    The following three views are centered at the same point \((1.5, f(1.5))\) on a graph. The solid curve is the graph of \(f(x)\text{.}\) The dashed line is the tangent line at that point. Each view is zoomed in more than the one before it.
    🔗

    (a)

    Describe what happens to the solid curve compared to the dashed line as we zoom in. Why is the dashed line a good description of the graph near the dot, but only when we are zoomed in?
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    In the views above, is the slope of the tangent line positive, negative, or zero? How can you tell without any numbers on the axes?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2. Section 2: Tangent slopes along a graph.

    Here is the graph of a function \(f(x)\) shown for \(-4 \le x \le 4\text{.}\) The dots mark the points where \(x\) is \(-4\text{,}\) \(-3\text{,}\) \(-1.5\text{,}\) \(0\text{,}\) \(1.5\text{,}\) \(3\text{,}\) and \(4\text{.}\) Each grid square is 1 unit wide and 1 unit tall, with lighter lines halfway between.
    🔗
    We will measure the slope of the tangent line at each dot, record the slopes in a table, and then plot them. Estimates are fine. Use a straightedge and draw each tangent line so that it matches the graph as closely as possible at its dot.

    3. Section 3: The derivative function.

    The curve you just sketched takes each \(x\)-value and reports the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f(x)\) at that \(x\)-value. That makes it a function in its own right, and it has a name.
    🔗

    The Derivative.

    The derivative of \(f(x)\) is the function \(f'(x)\) (read “\(f\) prime of \(x\)”) whose output at each \(x=a\) is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f(x)\) at the point \((a, f(a))\text{.}\) In particular, the graph of \(f(x)\) has a horizontal tangent line at \(x=a\) exactly when \(f'(a)=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    When we learned about the concavity of \(f(x)\text{,}\) we discussed the idea that \(f(x)\) is concave up when the slope is increasing. That is, \(f'(x)\) must increase on any interval where \(f(x)\) is concave up. Similarly, \(f'(x)\) must decrease on any interval where \(f(x)\) is concave down. Consequently, inflection points of \(f(x)\) correspond to turning points for \(f'(x)\text{,}\) where \(f'(x)\) switches between increasing and decreasing.
    🔗
    🔗
    The input of \(f'(x)\) is a position along the graph of \(f(x)\text{,}\) and its output is a slope. Read \(f'(3)\) as “the slope of the tangent line to \(f(x)\) at \(x=3\)”. Keep the two functions apart: \(f(3)\) is a height and \(f'(3)\) is a slope.
    🔗

    (a)

    Find the \(x\)-values where your sketch of \(f'(x)\) crosses the \(x\)-axis. Where do those \(x\)-values show up on the graph of \(f(x)\text{?}\) Explain the connection.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use your sketch of \(f'(x)\) to estimate \(f'(-2)\) and \(f'(3.5)\text{.}\) Write what each value tells you about the graph of \(f(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4. Section 4: When a tangent line does not exist.

    A tangent line to \(g(x)\) at \(x=a\) needs two things. There must be a point \((a, g(a))\) for the line to pass through, and the graph must look like a line when we zoom in. Here we study a graph with a point that is present but where the graph never looks like a line.
    🔗
    The graph has a sharp corner at \(x=0\text{.}\) No matter how far we zoom in on a corner, it still looks like a corner and never like a single line. The pieces on either side do each look like a line, though, so we can still ask about the slope that each side is heading toward. We write \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^-} g'(x)\) for the slope of the tangent line to the piece of the graph immediately to the left of \(x=0\text{,}\) and \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^+} g'(x)\) for the slope of the tangent line to the piece immediately to the right.
    🔗

    (a)

    On Figure 55, draw the tangent line to the left piece of the graph at the point \((0, 2)\text{,}\) and the tangent line to the right piece at the same point. Estimate their slopes and complete the statements.
    \begin{align*} \lim_{x \to 0^-} g'(x) \amp = \underline{\qquad\quad}\\ \lim_{x \to 0^+} g'(x) \amp = \underline{\qquad\quad} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Is there a single number that can be the slope of the tangent line to \(g(x)\) at \(x=0\text{?}\) Explain why \(g'(0)\) does not exist even though \(g(0)\) does.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Sketch \(g'(x)\) for \(-3 \lt x \lt 4\) on the axes below. Use the horizontal tangent lines to place the zeros of \(g'(x)\text{.}\) Estimate the slope at \(x=-3\) and at \(x=4\text{.}\) At \(x=0\text{,}\) mark the two one-sided values from above with open points.
